How a multi-camera live stream works

18 November 20265 min readBy Apex Video

A multi-camera live stream uses several cameras at once, with a director switching between them in real time, so people watching online see a finished programme: wide shots, close-ups and audience moments, cut as they happen. Here is what goes on behind it, and how to plan one for your event.

What is a multi-camera live stream?

A single-camera stream points one camera at the stage and leaves it there. It works for a short talk, but for a concert, a conference or an award show it quickly feels flat: you can't see the speaker's face and the slides at the same time, or the musician's hands and the whole ensemble.

A multi-camera stream fixes this. Each camera covers a different view, all of them feed into a video switcher, and a director chooses which view goes out to the audience at every moment. The result looks like television, not like a phone held up at the back of the hall.

Who is on the crew?

A typical crew has camera operators on each position, a director at the switcher, and someone responsible for audio and the stream itself. The sound comes from the venue's mixing desk rather than the camera microphones, so the online audience hears the same clean mix as the hall.

Everyone stays connected on wireless talkback. The director can ask one operator to tighten on the vocalist while another holds the wide shot, without a word being heard in the room.

What happens before the event?

Most of the work happens before anyone goes live:

  • The recce. We visit the venue to see the stage, the seating, the lighting and where cameras can stand without blocking anyone.
  • The internet connection. A stream is only as reliable as its connection. A wired line at the venue is far safer than shared Wi-Fi, and we check it well before the day.
  • The run of show. We go through the programme with the organiser so the director knows what is coming: the welcome, the keynote, the award segment, the encore.
  • The test. On the day, cameras, switcher, audio and talkback are set up and a test stream runs before doors open.

How does the director cut the cameras?

The director watches every camera at once on a multi-view monitor and decides, shot by shot, what the audience should see. For a speaker that might be a medium shot while they talk, a wide when they walk across the stage, and a cut to the audience when the room laughs or applauds.

Music needs more patience. A raga builds slowly, and the right moment to cut to the tabla player's hands matters as much as the wide shot of the stage. Calling these shots over talkback, live, is what separates a broadcast from a recording.

Where does the stream go?

The finished programme can go to YouTube, to your company's own event platform, or to a private link shared only with registered guests. The same feed can also drive the screens inside the hall, so people at the back see the speaker clearly.

What do you get after the event?

Everything that goes out live is also recorded. That recording can be edited into a highlight film, full-length sessions, or individual performances. For the Virasat festival in Dehradun, where we are the official videography partner, we edit each artist's performance song by song for YouTube, so one shoot keeps working long after the festival ends.

How to plan a live stream for your event

If you are organising an event in Mumbai or anywhere in India, these details help any production team give you an accurate proposal:

  • The date, the venue and the timings of the sessions you want streamed.
  • Where the stream should appear: YouTube, your own platform, or a private link.
  • Whether the venue has a wired internet connection available.
  • What you need afterwards: the full recording, a highlight film, or individual sessions.
